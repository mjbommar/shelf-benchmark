## Foreword and Scope

This handbook is a practice-oriented reference for specialists working at the intersection of human rights and international law in **Michigan** and **Italy**. It is written for attorneys, judges, legislative counsel, policy drafters, and in-house legal teams who routinely confront questions such as:

- What is the domestic effect of treaty obligations and customary international law?
- How do courts in Michigan (state and federal) and courts in Italy (ordinary and constitutional) operationalize human rights norms?
- How should counsel build a record and frame arguments when international materials are persuasive but not dispositive?
- What remedies are realistically obtainable, and what procedural barriers commonly defeat claims?

The focus is practical: **jurisdiction-specific procedures**, **pleading standards**, **discovery practice**, **evidentiary rules**, and **appellate review**. For each topic, we provide side-by-side analysis—Michigan law in one column, Italian law in the other—so that counsel can quickly compare doctrines, cite relevant authorities, and adapt strategies developed in one system for use in the other.

### Why Michigan and Italy?

1. **Comparable constitutional frameworks** but different enforcement mechanisms.
2. **Active NGO litigation** using international instruments (ICCPR, ECHR analogs).
3. **Federalism issues**: U.S. state courts and Italian regional variations complicate the application of supranational norms.
4. **Practical cooperation**: Both jurisdictions collaborate on extradition, mutual legal assistance, and transnational rights cases.

This handbook does not duplicate general treaties on international human rights law. Instead, it assumes the reader is familiar with core instruments (UDHR, ICCPR, ICESCR, European Convention on Human Rights) and seeks **jurisdiction-specific implementation guidance**.

## Part I — Sources of Law and Hierarchy

### A. Michigan (United States)

#### Federal System Overview

The United States is a federal republic. Human rights claims may arise under:

1. **U.S. Constitution** (Bill of Rights, 14th Amendment Due Process and Equal Protection).
2. **Federal statutes** (Civil Rights Act of 1964, Americans with Disabilities Act, etc.).
3. **Treaties** ratified by the Senate (ICCPR, Convention Against Torture, etc.).
4. **Customary international law** (recognized as federal common law in limited contexts; see *Filartiga v. Peña-Irala*, 630 F.2d 876 (2d Cir. 1980)).

**State law** in Michigan:

- Michigan Constitution (Article I, Declaration of Rights).
- Michigan Civil Rights Act (Elliott-Larsen, MCL 37.2101 *et seq.*).
- Common-law tort claims (intentional infliction of emotional distress, assault, false imprisonment).

#### Hierarchy and Supremacy

Under the Supremacy Clause (U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2):

1. **Self-executing treaties** have the force of federal law and preempt contrary state law.
2. **Non-self-executing treaties** require implementing legislation; courts will not enforce them directly (*Medellín v. Texas*, 552 U.S. 491 (2008)).
3. **Customary international law** may inform interpretation of constitutional or statutory ambiguities but does not, on its own, create a private right of action.

**Practice tip**: In Michigan state courts, argue treaty provisions through the lens of the Michigan Constitution's due process clause (Art. I, § 17) or by analogy to parallel federal rights. Federal district courts (E.D. Mich., W.D. Mich.) have jurisdiction over claims arising under federal law, including treaties, but be prepared for dismissal if the treaty is deemed non-self-executing.

### B. Italy

#### Monist Orientation

Italy follows a **monist** approach: international law is automatically incorporated into the domestic legal order upon ratification, subject to constitutional review.

**Sources**:

1. **Italian Constitution** (Costituzione della Repubblica Italiana, 1948).
   - Art. 10: Automatic incorporation of customary international law.
   - Art. 117, para. 1: Regional and national legislation must comply with EU law and international obligations.
2. **European Convention on Human Rights** (ECHR), incorporated via Law No. 848/1955.
   - Direct effect; enforceable in Italian courts.
   - Constitutional Court may review domestic law for ECHR compliance (*Corte Cost. sent. n. 348 and 349/2007*).
3. **European Union law** (Treaties, Regulations, Directives).
   - Primacy over national law (*Corte Cost. sent. n. 170/1984*, "Granital").
   - Charter of Fundamental Rights (CFR) applies when Italy implements EU law.

**Hierarchy** (simplified):

1. Italian Constitution (supreme).
2. EU primary law (Treaties) — treated as quasi-constitutional (*controlimiti* doctrine allows Constitutional Court to reject EU norms that violate core constitutional principles).
3. ECHR and customary international law (sub-constitutional but supra-legislative).
4. Statutes (Codice Civile, Codice Penale, etc.).
5. Regulations and administrative acts.

**Practice tip**: Frame human rights arguments under **Art. 117 Cost.** + ECHR, not just domestic statutes. The Constitutional Court has invalidated laws for ECHR violations. In ordinary courts (Tribunale, Corte d'Appello), judges are required to disapply conflicting statutes in favor of ECHR norms (*sent. Cass. S.U. n. 6905/2012*).

In tracing visual practices across Asia and Paris, it is useful to treat artworks not only as aesthetic outcomes but as propositions about perception, selfhood, and the built world. Any such account is necessarily selective; "Asia" is not a single tradition, and "Paris" often functions as a metonym for institutions—academies, salons, museums—that concentrate visibility and authority (Clifford, 1988).

**Painting and the ethics of attention.** Classical Chinese literati painting is frequently interpreted as prioritizing cultivated seeing over mimetic accuracy, with brushwork indexing character and moral self-formation (Bush & Shih, 1985). While this framing risks idealizing elite practices, it illuminates a philosophical claim: the image can be evidence of a life disciplined by attention. In Kyoto and Edo-period Japan, ink painting (suiboku-ga) and the tea-ceremony aesthetic (wabi-sabi) likewise embed philosophical commitments—impermanence, restraint, the beauty of incompleteness—within visual and material culture (Keene, 1988).

Paris, by contrast, institutionalized a different set of assumptions through the Academy. History painting, as the apex of the hierarchy of genres, was justified by its capacity to instruct: it depicted virtue, heroism, and civic order, rendering moral and political philosophy visible (Crow, 1985). The academic tradition thus treats the image as a rhetorical instrument, not an index of the painter's inner cultivation but a public argument addressed to an educated viewer.

**Modernity and the crisis of representation.** The tension between these frameworks becomes acute in the late nineteenth century. Impressionism, post-Impressionism, and early modernism destabilize the assumption that the image is either a transparent record or a didactic narrative. Instead, the canvas becomes a site for investigating the **conditions** of seeing itself—light, color, time, subjective experience (Crary, 1990). Japanese woodblock prints (ukiyo-e), circulating widely in Paris after 1860, offered European artists an alternative spatial logic: flattened picture planes, asymmetrical composition, cropped forms. Artists such as Monet, Degas, and van Gogh adapted these devices, not as "exotic" decoration but as a means to challenge perspectival space and academic conventions (Wichmann, 1981).

This is not straightforward influence but a **selective appropriation**. Edo printmakers worked within a commercial and popular culture—kabuki, courtesans, travel—that had little to do with Parisian debates about "art for art's sake." To repurpose ukiyo-e conventions within the Paris gallery system is to **recontextualize** them, often stripping away their original social meanings. The ethics of this appropriation remain contested, particularly when we consider how "japonisme" functioned within European imperialism (Said, 1978; Orientalism).
